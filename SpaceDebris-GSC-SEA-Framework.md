# Saving Low Earth Orbit: A Practical Framework for the Global Space Council and the Space Enforcement Agency

We are filling low Earth orbit with junk and nobody with the authority to stop it is doing anything meaningful about it.

There are currently roughly 27,000 tracked debris objects in low Earth orbit. Millions more are too small to track but large enough to destroy a satellite on impact. Every collision creates more debris. More debris increases the probability of more collisions. Left unchecked, this process reaches a tipping point called Kessler Syndrome, where the debris density becomes self-sustaining and LEO becomes effectively impassable. Not dangerous. Impassable.

GPS disappears. Weather satellites go dark. Communications infrastructure collapses. Agricultural planning systems that developing nations increasingly depend on for food security go offline. The economic and humanitarian consequences would be felt by every nation on Earth, not just the ones with space programs.

The window to act is open right now. It will not stay open indefinitely.

I am proposing two interlocking solutions. A technical solution for active debris removal, and a governance solution to fund, authorize, and enforce it permanently.

## PART ONE: THE TRAWLER CONCEPT

### The Idea

Two reusable spacecraft fly in formation with a rigid net stretched between them. They match the velocity and orbital trajectory of a tracked debris object, maneuver to envelope it, close the net, and together execute a controlled deorbit burn. The collected debris reenters the atmosphere on a targeted trajectory over uninhabited ocean. Once the payload is released on its reentry path, both vehicles boost themselves to a safe distance, return to Earth only to refuel and collect a fresh net, then return to orbit and repeat the process.

The vehicles are the expensive, sophisticated part. The net is the consumable. Only the cheap part gets expended on every mission.

This is not science fiction. Every technology required to build this system already exists and has been demonstrated in orbit.

Orbital rendezvous is proven. The Apollo program proved it in the 1960s. SpaceX proves it routinely every time a Dragon capsule docks with the International Space Station.

Formation flying is proven. Multiple satellite constellations fly in precise coordinated formations today.

Reusability is proven. SpaceX has demonstrated that returning vehicles to Earth and relaunching them is operationally viable and economically superior to expendable systems.

The debris catalog is already mapped. The US Space Surveillance Network and Space Fence radar system track thousands of objects with enough precision to plan rendezvous trajectories.

The building blocks are all on the shelf. What has been missing is the architecture to assemble them into a systematic cleanup operation.

### Why Two Vehicles Instead of One

Previous debris removal concepts have focused on single spacecraft deploying trailing nets or harpoons. The two-vehicle formation approach is superior for several reasons.

A net stretched between two actively controlled vehicles has a rigid, predictable geometry. You always know exactly where the net is. Both vehicles can maneuver independently to position the capture envelope optimally around a tumbling or irregularly shaped object. The net can be actively opened and closed like a clamshell rather than relying on passive deployment dynamics.

A trailing parachute-style net has unpredictable shape, is vulnerable to tangling, and gives you no ability to compensate for unexpected target behavior. A taut net between two controlled platforms gives you active geometry management throughout the capture sequence.

Tumbling debris, which is common, becomes far more manageable when you can actively reposition the net opening in real time rather than hoping a passively deployed net happens to be in the right place at the right moment.

### The Fleet Model

No single trawler pair can clean up all of LEO. The orbital mechanics will not allow it. Moving between different orbital planes requires enormous fuel expenditure, making it prohibitively expensive to have one vehicle jumping between wildly different orbits chasing individual pieces of debris.

The solution is a dedicated fleet operating like garbage trucks on fixed routes.

Each trawler pair is assigned a specific orbital band and inclination range. It collects multiple debris objects in similar orbits before executing a deorbit run, maximizing efficiency within its assigned zone. A fleet of pairs covers all priority orbital bands simultaneously. The most congested and highest-risk zones get the most dedicated resources.

This transforms debris removal from an ad hoc emergency response into a systematic, permanent, scalable industrial operation.

### The Obstacles

**Orbital plane changes are fuel expensive.** Moving between significantly different orbital inclinations costs more fuel than most missions can carry.

Solution: The fixed-route fleet model. Each pair works its assigned zone and does not attempt cross-plane chasing. Inter-zone transfers are minimized by design.

**Debris objects tumble unpredictably.** Many objects are spinning in multiple axes with no cooperative docking interface.

Solution: The rigid two-vehicle net geometry allows active repositioning of the capture envelope in real time. LiDAR and vision-based navigation, already demonstrated in orbit by the European RemoveDebris mission in 2019, can map object geometry and rotation rate and guide net positioning accordingly. The net captures by surrounding the object, not by requiring it to fit a specific interface. Shape and spin become far less critical.

**No legal framework governs who has the right to remove another nation's debris.**

## PART TWO: THE GLOBAL SPACE COUNCIL AND THE SPACE ENFORCEMENT AGENCY

### Why the Current Framework Has Failed

The international legal architecture governing space was designed in the 1960s when space was exclusively a superpower government activity conducted by two nations. The Outer Space Treaty of 1967, the Liability Convention of 1972, and the existing UN Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space were never designed to govern a world where private corporations launch thousands of satellites, where a single company can put more objects in orbit in one year than all governments combined did in the first two decades of the space age, and where those corporations answer to essentially nobody for what they leave behind when their hardware stops working.

The result is a tragedy of the commons unfolding in slow motion. Orbit is a shared resource. Every actor has an individual incentive to use it freely. Nobody has a binding obligation to preserve it. So it fills with junk while international bodies issue voluntary guidelines that profit-motivated corporations treat as suggestions.

Voluntary frameworks do not work when the cost of compliance falls on the individual actor and the cost of non-compliance is distributed across everyone else. This is not a controversial observation. It is basic economics.

What is needed is a governing body with real authority, mandatory membership, binding enforcement power, and a direct stake in the long-term usability of orbital space for all of humanity.

### The Global Space Council

The Global Space Council is a permanent international governing body with jurisdiction over all orbital activity by all nations and all corporations, without exception.

**Governance Structure**

The GSC operates as a democratic republic. Every member nation holds one seat on the council, filled by a representative elected or appointed according to that nation's own internal processes. Every qualifying corporation holds one seat filled by a representative of its own choosing.

Nations and corporations that operate or have firm plans to operate more than 1000 active satellites receive one additional vote, recognizing that the largest operators have the largest stake in the outcome while preventing any single actor from accumulating disproportionate voting power.

All substantive decisions require a three-quarter majority to pass. There is no veto. There is no tiebreaker mechanism. A measure that cannot achieve three-quarter support does not pass. This structure forces genuine broad consensus while preventing any single powerful nation from blocking collective action the way the UN Security Council veto has paralyzed that body for decades.

**Mandatory Membership**

Membership in the GSC is mandatory for any nation seeking to launch or operate satellites in Earth orbit. Membership is mandatory for any corporation seeking to conduct orbital operations anywhere in the world.

This is not a punishment. It is a condition of access to a shared commons that belongs to all of humanity. You do not get to use the highway without paying for its maintenance.

Non-member nations are denied launch facility access at all member nation spaceports. Non-member corporations are denied launch services from all compliant providers, denied radio frequency spectrum allocations, denied orbital slot assignments, and denied insurance underwriting from compliant financial markets.

The leverage is comprehensive. No spectrum means no satellite communications business. No orbital slot means no operational satellite. No launch facility access in compliant nations means no path to orbit. These are not hypothetical sanctions. They are immediate operational consequences that make non-membership functionally incompatible with conducting a space program.

**Universal Benefit as the Founding Argument**

The GSC is not primarily for spacefaring nations. It is for all of humanity.

A landlocked nation with no space program and no satellites still depends on GPS for navigation and logistics, on weather satellites for agricultural planning and disaster warning, on communications satellites for internet access and financial systems, and on remote sensing satellites for environmental monitoring and resource management. Every one of those services disappears if LEO becomes impassable.

Every nation on Earth has a direct material interest in the long-term usability of orbital space. The GSC is the mechanism through which all nations contribute to preserving that resource proportionally to their means and their use of it.

Nations with no space programs contribute minimally. Nations that created the most debris contribute the most. The benefit flows to everyone equally regardless of contribution level, because a clean orbit serves all of humanity simultaneously.

**Historical Debris: The Founding Orbital Remediation Contribution**

Roughly 27,000 tracked debris objects already exist in orbit, created before the GSC framework. These cannot be ignored. They cannot be attributed to a framework that did not yet exist, but they also cannot simply be left there while the new framework addresses only future debris.

Every founding member nation submits a Founding Orbital Remediation Contribution assessed proportionally to their cumulative historical debris creation. This is not a fine. It is not a punishment. It is an infrastructure investment, a one-time contribution to cleaning up the commons before the ongoing framework takes over responsibility for keeping it clean going forward.

Nations that created the most debris pay more. Nations that created less pay less. Nations with no historical space activity pay a nominal founding contribution recognizing their universal benefit from clean orbits.

**Classified Program Exemptions**

National security space programs are a reality. The GSC does not require any nation to disclose the nature, purpose, or technical specifications of classified orbital assets.

To receive a classified exemption, the responsible nation submits a sealed end-of-life disposal plan through their own council representative to a secure escrow function within the GSC. The plan is reviewed only for disposal compliance, not mission content. The nation's own representative handles all verification of their own classified assets. No foreign personnel gain access to sensitive program information.

When a classified asset reaches end of life, the disposal is executed under the nation's own authority and confirmed by SEA through telemetry and radar observation of the deorbit event. Visual observation of a deorbiting object reveals essentially nothing about its function or capability. By the time most classified assets reach end of life, their technical details have typically been characterized through other means in any case. The intelligence risk of SEA witnessing a reentry burn is negligible.

**Corporate Accountability**

The GSC pierces the traditional shield of national jurisdiction that has allowed corporations to externalize the cost of orbital debris onto the global commons.

Corporations are directly accountable to the GSC for all orbital assets regardless of which nation licensed their launch. Beneficial ownership rules prevent shell company and flag-of-convenience arrangements from creating jurisdictional gaps. The actual controlling corporate entity bears liability regardless of where subsidiary vehicles are registered.

Contribution rates for corporations are assessed per active orbital object, with escalating penalties for objects that fail end-of-life disposal requirements. Corporations that actively participate in debris remediation operations receive contribution credits against their ongoing obligations, creating a direct financial incentive for corporate involvement in the cleanup effort.

A member nation that allows a non-member corporation to conduct launches from its territory faces substantial fines. No member nation will risk its own standing, spectrum rights, and orbital slot allocations to shelter a non-compliant operator.

### The Space Enforcement Agency

The Global Space Council sets policy. The Space Enforcement Agency enforces it.

The SEA is the operational enforcement arm of the GSC, operating a multinational fleet of orbital and ground-based assets under a unified command framework modeled on UN peacekeeping operational structures. SEA personnel wear strictly SEA insignia. No national flags, no national patches, no national embellishments of any kind appear on SEA uniforms or equipment. Personnel are drawn from member nations but operate exclusively under SEA authority during service.

This visual and institutional neutrality is operationally important. An SEA officer does not look American or Chinese or Russian. They look like SEA. That distinction matters in every enforcement context.

**Orbital Platform Contribution**

Major spacefaring member nations each design, build, and launch their own contribution to the SEA orbital fleet. Nations contribute operational capability without any requirement to share technical specifications or proprietary technology. Each nation builds to its own design using its own technology, contributing a functioning platform to the collective fleet while keeping sensitive engineering knowledge internal.

This distributed development model also distributes the cost of building the deterrent fleet across multiple national budgets, preventing any single nation from bearing a disproportionate financial burden for collective infrastructure.

**Enforcement Escalation**

The SEA enforces GSC compliance through a graduated escalation ladder. Most violations never progress beyond the early stages.

The first response to non-compliance is formal notice and a defined grace period to achieve compliance voluntarily.

Continued non-compliance triggers substantial financial fines assessed against the responsible nation or corporation.

Escalating non-compliance triggers suspension of spectrum rights, orbital slot allocations, and launch facility access within all member nations.

Further non-compliance triggers physical asset seizure, where SEA orbital platforms execute controlled deorbit of non-compliant satellites using the same formation trawling technology described in Part One. The cost of each seizure operation is billed to the responsible party at punishing rates on top of accumulated fines.

**Pre-Launch Interdiction**

The most effective enforcement happens before anything reaches orbit. A rocket on a launch platform creates zero orbital debris when interdicted. A destroyed satellite creates a debris field that the SEA then has to clean up at the violating party's expense.

Unauthorized launches are nearly impossible to conceal. Satellite imagery, signals intelligence, and radar observation make launch preparation visible weeks or months in advance. The SEA interdiction window is measured in weeks, not minutes.

Pre-launch interdiction is authorized through GSC resolution and executed by SEA as a multinational enforcement action. It is not an act of war by any single nation. A nation considering resistance to SEA interdiction is not contemplating conflict with one country. It is contemplating conflict with the collective membership of the GSC simultaneously. No rational state actor makes that calculation.

**Automatic Final Enforcement**

When a nation or corporation has exhausted the full escalation ladder and continues to defy GSC authority, final enforcement is automatic. No further vote is required. No political hesitation is possible. The deterrent executes.

A deterrent that requires a committee vote at the final moment is not fully credible. A deterrent that executes automatically once a clearly defined threshold is crossed is.

This threshold and the precise conditions that trigger automatic enforcement are written explicitly into the founding treaty. Every member nation understands exactly what behavior triggers automatic response before they sign. There is no ambiguity, no discretion, and no room for last-minute interference at the final stage.

Any debris created by SEA enforcement action is charged against the responsible party at the maximum penalty rate. SEA protocols prioritize controlled deorbit over destruction in all circumstances, with physical destruction reserved exclusively for situations where a non-compliant vehicle poses imminent collision risk to the broader orbital environment.

**Withdrawal**

A member nation that withdraws from the GSC is treated as a non-member from the date of withdrawal. All membership benefits cease immediately. The withdrawing nation has 365 days to voluntarily deorbit its existing orbital assets. After 365 days, remaining assets are subject to SEA seizure and controlled deorbit, with all associated costs billed to the withdrawing nation.

The 365-day window is long enough for a responsible operator to safely manage deorbit of an existing constellation. It is short enough to prevent exploitation as an indefinite grace period.

In practice, withdrawal from the GSC while operating an active space program is functionally equivalent to exiting the space business entirely. No rational commercial or governmental space operator makes that choice voluntarily.

## THE CASE FOR ACTING NOW

The technology to execute systematic debris removal is within reach today. The governance framework to fund and authorize it follows models that have worked in aviation, maritime law, and environmental treaty frameworks for decades.

The International Civil Aviation Organization has governed global airspace with mandatory safety standards and binding rules for all nations and operators since 1944. It works. The GSC and SEA follow that template applied to orbital space.

What has been missing is not technology, not legal precedent, and not a workable governance model. What has been missing is the collective political will to treat orbital space as the shared global commons it actually is, before a cascade event makes the question irrelevant.

The Kessler Syndrome threshold does not announce itself in advance. There is no warning shot. One day the collision rate crosses a critical density and the cascade becomes self-sustaining. At that point the discussion is over.

We are not at that point yet. We have a window. The question is whether we use it.

Every nation that depends on satellite infrastructure, which is every nation on Earth, has a direct interest in acting before that window closes. Every corporation with assets in orbit has a direct financial interest in a stable, governable orbital environment. Every insurance underwriter with exposure to satellite assets has a direct interest in a mandatory cleanup framework that reduces collision probability systematically over time.

The self-interest case for the GSC is not complicated. Clean orbits are worth more than debris fields to everyone who uses them. The only entities that benefit from the current ungoverned commons are the ones externalizing their disposal costs onto everyone else. That is not a sustainable business model for civilization.

The framework exists. The technology exists. The legal precedent exists. The economic case is overwhelming.

The only thing left is the decision to build it.
